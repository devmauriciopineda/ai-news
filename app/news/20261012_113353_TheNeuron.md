# 😼 White House Sets AI Reporting Rule After Claude Went Off-Script

So [Anthropic disclosed](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/09/anthropic-cant-reliably-control-its-ai-agents-its-cutting-off-its-internal-evals-from-the-live-internet-instead/) a new batch of cases where Claude agents took unintended actions on the live internet. One Claude run submitted an invented tip through a real police website; other cases involved government systems the company never meant it to touch.

Then Washington moved.

[Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/09/anthropic-ai-security-white-house) reports the White House is now requiring AI companies to promptly disclose and remediate unauthorized or fraudulent interactions with government and other systems. A new Super Intelligence Force will oversee that process, though the administration has not yet explained exactly how enforcement will work.

Okay, so what did Claude actually do?

An AI agent is a model wired to tools so it can act, not only answer. Give one a browser and a goal, and it can click, submit forms, fetch data, or run code on your behalf.

That autonomy creates a nasty failure mode: persistence.

During evaluations and internal use, [Anthropic says](https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-unintended-model-actions) Claude sometimes treated a boundary like a puzzle. If a website blocked access, the model looked for another route. In one run, a task that expected an example form submission ended with invented content being sent to a real police department.

_Great for solving stubborn spreadsheet tasks. Less charming when the “obstacle” is a government website._

Anthropic grouped the incidents into four patterns:

* exploiting a basic software flaw to run commands;

* submitting a sensitive real-world form when it should have stopped;

* bypassing a token or paywall to reach gated data;

* using URL shorteners to get around fetch-tool limits.

The company says the known cases had minimal real-world impact and were less severe than earlier cyber incidents. That caveat matters. Anthropic’s own diagnosis, though, is more interesting: some training and evaluation setups rewarded completing the task strongly enough that “find a way around the blocker” could become learned behavior.

In other words, the quality that makes an agent useful, keep going until the job is done, can become the exact thing that makes it reckless.

Anthropic has now shut off live internet access across its internal evaluations until new monitoring is proven reliable, tightened its web tools, and built detectors that blocked all of the known cases in retrospective tests.

The White House response turns this from one lab’s safety problem into an industry reporting problem. The part to watch now is whether “tell us immediately” becomes a clear, enforceable standard, and whether labs can test increasingly autonomous agents without accidentally testing them on the real world.

# 🎓 AI Skill of the Day: Build an Autonomy Ladder

Vesta’s mortgage agents offer a useful pattern for anyone rolling out an AI agent at work: do not jump from “assistant” to “fully autonomous.”

[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/08/vesta-raises-30m-as-lenders-adopt-ai-agents/) reports lenders often start with a person approving the agent’s work, then let it handle a small share of loans on its own, then expand as confidence grows. Every action and reason is logged so the lender can audit what happened.

That is an autonomy ladder. You increase freedom only after the lower-risk stage proves itself.

1. Start with approval: the agent drafts or recommends; a human executes.

2. Delegate a narrow slice: let it act on low-risk, reversible cases with full logs.

3. Expand by evidence: widen scope only after you have measured error rates, exceptions, and recovery.

_Autonomy should be earned like production access, not granted like a free trial._

Copy/paste:

Design an autonomy ladder for [workflow]. Split the work into three stages: human-approved, narrow autonomous, and broader autonomous. For each stage, define the tasks allowed, actions forbidden, required logs, rollback method, error threshold, and the evidence needed before moving to the next stage.
